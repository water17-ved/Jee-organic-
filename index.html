Vinyl (CH₂=CH⁺) & Aryl cations are extremely unstable — essentially inaccessible under normal conditions.

🔑 Why 3° > 2° > 1°?

Hyperconjugation (C–H σ → empty p) and inductive electron donation from alkyl groups delocalise the positive charge. More alkyl groups = more hyperconjugation = greater stability.

⚡ Allylic / Benzylic Boost

Resonance delocalises charge over π system. Allylic: two contributing structures. Benzylic: four+ structures around the ring. A benzylic tertiary cation is the most stable common intermediate.

🔄 Rearrangements

Carbocations rearrange to a more stable isomer via 1,2-hydride shifts or 1,2-alkyl shifts (Wagner–Meerwein). Always check if a rearrangement is possible in JEE problems!

📐 Geometry

sp² planar. The empty p-orbital is perpendicular to the plane — allows overlap with adjacent π bonds (allylic/benzylic). This is why bridging locks disrupt stability in anti-Bredt ions.

🧪 JEE Trap: Vinyl Cations

Vinyl cations are sp hybridised (linear). The positive charge is in a sp orbital — not delocalised by the π bond. Hence they are far less stable than expected and almost never form in SN1.

🌟 Non-classical Ions

e.g. 2-norbornyl cation — stabilised by σ-bridging (σ-participation). Not a focus of JEE Main, but does appear in Advanced context questions.

Memory Order
Methyl < 1° < 2° < 3° < Allylic ≈ Benzylic < Tropylium (C₇H₇⁺)

Tropylium ion is aromatic (6π electrons on a 7-membered ring) — the most stable common carbocation.

Enolate

Alkyl groups are electron-donating (destabilise carbanion). EWG (C=O, CN, NO₂) adjacent to carbanion strongly stabilise it via resonance & induction.

🔑 Why opposite to carbocations?

Alkyl groups donate electrons. For carbocations this helps (disperses +charge). For carbanions it hurts (adds more –charge). Hence the order reverses.

📡 Hybridisation Effect

sp > sp² > sp³ in terms of carbanion stability. More s-character = electrons held closer to nucleus = more stable. Acetylide (sp) > vinyl (sp²) > alkyl (sp³). This is why terminal alkynes are acidic (pKₐ ≈ 25).

🌀 EWG Stabilisation

–CN, –NO₂, –COR, –COOR adjacent to carbanion allow resonance delocalisation of negative charge onto electronegative atoms (N, O). Each additional EWG lowers pKₐ significantly.

🧪 Key Acidity Order (pKₐ)

RC≡CH (≈25) < ArH (≈43) < R₂C=CH₂ (≈44) < RCH₂–R (≈50). Lower pKₐ = more stable conjugate base (carbanion).

3° < 2° < 1° < Methyl < Allylic ≈ Benzylic < α-carbonyl (Enolate) < Acetylide

🔑 Parallel with Carbocations

The stability trend mirrors carbocations: hyperconjugation and resonance still operate on the half-filled orbital. The stabilisation is weaker than for the full empty orbital, but the trend is the same.

⚙️ Selectivity vs Reactivity

Cl• is highly reactive → low selectivity (attacks 1°, 2°, 3° almost equally). Br• is less reactive → high selectivity (strongly prefers 3° > 2° >> 1°). More stable TS = more selective radical.

🌀 Allylic Radicals

The unpaired electron is delocalised across three carbons (resonance). NBS (N-bromosuccinimide) generates allylic radicals selectively. Used to make allylic bromides in synthesis.

🧪 Persistent Radicals

Triphenylmethyl radical (Ph₃C•) is stable enough to exist in solution due to extensive benzylic resonance and steric protection. A classic JEE Advanced example of a stable free radical.

🔗 Chain Mechanism

Initiation (X₂ → 2X•) → Propagation (X• + RH → HX + R•; R• + X₂ → RX + X•) → Termination (radical + radical → product). Each step has defined ΔH.

📊 BDE & Radical Stability

Lower BDE of C–H = more stable radical formed on homolysis. BDE (kcal/mol): CH₃–H 105 > 1°–H 100 > 2°–H 98.5 > 3°–H 96.5 > Allylic–H 88 > Benzylic–H 90.

Methyl < 1° < 2° < 3° < Allylic ≈ Benzylic < Diphenylmethyl < Triphenylmethyl
Carbenes (:CR₂)

Neutral carbon with only 6 electrons and two non-bonding electrons. Generated by α-elimination or photolysis. Extremely reactive electrophiles.

🔬 Singlet Carbene

Both non-bonding electrons in the same sp² orbital (paired, ↑↓). sp² hybridised. More reactive, electrophilic. Gives stereospecific syn addition to alkenes (cis-alkene → cis-cyclopropane).

🌀 Triplet Carbene

Two electrons in different orbitals with parallel spins (↑↑). Diradical character. Less reactive. Gives non-stereospecific addition (both cis and trans products). Usually the ground state.

⚗️ Generation

CHCl₃ + KOH → :CCl₂ (dichlorocarbene). CH₂N₂ hν → :CH₂ + N₂ (methylene). Also from Simmons–Smith reagent (carbenoid). :CCl₂ is singlet; :CH₂ is triplet in ground state.

🔑 Key Reactions

① Cyclopropanation of alkenes (syn addition for singlet). ② Insertion into C–H bonds. ③ Rearrangement. ④ Reaction with O₂ (triplet). Simmons–Smith uses carbenoid (:CH₂ from Zn/Cu) for stereospecific cyclopropanation.

📐 Structure

Singlet: sp² bent (~100–110°). Triplet: sp² or sp, bent or linear depending on substituents. Lone pair occupies sp² orbital in singlet; half-filled orbitals in triplet are sp² and p.

🧪 JEE Focus

Most commonly tested: dichlorocarbene from CHCl₃/KOH, its electrophilic syn addition to alkenes giving gem-dihalocyclopropanes. Stereochemistry of the alkene is retained in the product.

CHCl₃ + KOH → :CCl₂ → alkene → 1,1-dihalocyclopropane (syn)
Nitrenes (R–N:)

Nitrogen analogues of carbenes — a nitrogen atom with only 6 electrons. Highly reactive, electrophilic. Generated by thermolysis or photolysis of azides or nitroso compounds.

🔬 Formation

R–N₃ (hν or Δ) → R–N: + N₂. Also from Hofmann bromamide reaction (intermediate is nitrene-like acyl nitrene). Curtius and Lossen rearrangements involve nitrene intermediates.

⚗️ Reactions

① Insertion into C–H bonds (like carbenes). ② Addition to π bonds → aziridines. ③ Rearrangement — acyl nitrenes rearrange to isocyanates (Curtius: RCO–N: → R–N=C=O).

🔄 Key Rearrangements Involving Nitrenes

Hofmann: RCONH₂ + Br₂/KOH → RNH₂ (nitrene-type). Curtius: RCOCl + NaN₃ → RCON₃ → R–N=C=O → RNH₂. Schmidt: Carboxylic acid + HN₃ → isocyanate → amine.

🧪 JEE Focus

Primarily tested in context of Hofmann, Curtius, Lossen, and Schmidt rearrangements. Remember: all these convert RCOX → RNH₂ (one carbon less). Nitrene is the mechanistic intermediate.

R–CO–N: (acyl nitrene) → [1,2-shift] → R–N=C=O (isocyanate) → + H₂O → RNH₂
Arynes (Benzynes)

Highly strained ring intermediates with a formal triple bond in a benzene ring. Generated by strong base + aryl halide (elimination). Key in nucleophilic aromatic substitution via elimination–addition.

🔬 Structure

Benzyne (1,2-didehydrobenzene): a 6-membered ring with an additional π bond in the plane of the ring using sp² orbital overlap. Extremely strained and reactive. Cannot be isolated.

⚗️ Generation

ArX + strong base (NaNH₂, n-BuLi) → ArX loses HX → benzyne. Alternatively, anthranilic acid → benzyne + CO₂ + N₂ (thermolysis of diazonium salt). Also: fluorobenzene + n-BuLi.

🔑 Elimination–Addition Mechanism

Base abstracts ortho-H → benzyne → nucleophile adds (can add at either carbon of the triple bond) → product mixture. Explains why halobenzene + NaOH/high T gives both ortho-substituted and meta products — actually it's the benzyne migrating.

🧪 JEE Trap: Isotope Labelling

Classic JEE Advanced test: chlorobenzene-1-¹⁴C + NaNH₂ → aniline with ¹⁴C at C1 and C2 equally (50:50). Proves benzyne intermediate — Nu can attack either end of the strained triple bond.

⚡ Reactivity

Acts as a dienophile in Diels–Alder with dienes (useful in synthesis). Reacts with nucleophiles by addition (not substitution). Can also dimerize at very low temperatures.

C₆H₅Cl + NaNH₂ → [C₆H₄] (benzyne) + NaCl → + NH₃ → C₆H₅NH₂ (aniline)
Enolates & Enols

Stabilised carbanions formed α to a carbonyl. The most important synthetic intermediates in JEE — central to aldol, Claisen, Michael, Knoevenagel, and many named reactions.

🔄 Enol–Enolate Relationship

Enol: neutral tautomer of a ketone (OH on sp² carbon). Enolate: conjugate base (O⁻, charge delocalised to α-C). Base deprotonates α to C=O to give enolate. Enolate is the nucleophile; enol is the neutral form.

⚖️ Kinetic vs Thermodynamic Enolate

Kinetic (LDA, −78°C, fast): deprotonates the less hindered (less substituted) α-carbon. Thermodynamic (NaOEt, equilibrating): gives more substituted (more stable) enolate. JEE Advanced tests this distinction.

📊 Enolate Stability

More EWG adjacent = more stable enolate (more acidic α-H). Malonic ester (pKₐ ≈ 13) > acetoacetic ester (pKₐ ≈ 11) > simple ketone (pKₐ ≈ 20) > ester (pKₐ ≈ 25) > amide (pKₐ ≈ 30).

⚗️ Alkylation

Enolate + R–X (SN2) → α-alkylated product. Malonic ester synthesis: allows introduction of R group, then decarboxylation gives carboxylic acid (RCH₂COOH). Acetoacetic ester synthesis: gives methyl ketones.

🔗 Aldol Condensation

Enolate (nucleophile) attacks aldehyde/ketone carbonyl (electrophile) → β-hydroxy carbonyl (aldol product) → dehydration (Δ or base) → α,β-unsaturated carbonyl (aldol condensation product).

🌀 Michael Addition

Enolate (Michael donor, 1,4-addition) adds to α,β-unsaturated carbonyl (Michael acceptor) at the β-carbon. Combines with aldol to give Robinson annulation — the core of steroid synthesis. High-yield JEE topic.

pKₐ of α–H (Approximate)
Malonate (13) < Acetoacetate (11) < CHCl₃ (25) < Ketone (20) < Ester (25) < Amide (30) < Alkane (50)

Lower pKₐ = more acidic α-H = more stable enolate. Two flanking C=O groups dramatically lower pKₐ (malonate, acetylacetone).
