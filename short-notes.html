4. Hydrocarbons — Key Facts

  • Free-radical halogenation reactivity of C–H bonds: 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₄; but selectivity is highest for bromination and lowest for chlorination/fluorination (F₂ is almost unselective and explosive).
  • Halogen reactivity towards free-radical substitution: F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂ (I₂ substitution is reversible/non-feasible without an oxidiser).
  • Knocking tendency of fuels decreases in the order: straight-chain alkanes > branched alkanes > alkenes > aromatics/branched — tetraethyl lead (TEL) is used as an anti-knock additive.
  • Alkenes: Markovnikov addition (HX) gives the more stable carbocation product; peroxide effect reverses HBr addition only (anti-Markovnikov, via free-radical mechanism), never with HCl or HI.
  • Alkynes: terminal alkynes are acidic (pKa ≈ 25) and give a red precipitate with ammoniacal Cu₂Cl₂ or a white precipitate with ammoniacal AgNO₃ — non-terminal alkynes do not.
  • Ozonolysis of an alkyne gives a diketone or diacid, requiring two equivalents of ozone (one more than the corresponding alkene).

5. Haloalkanes & Grignard Reagent

  • SN1 vs SN2: 3° halides go SN1 (racemisation, rearrangement possible); 1° halides go SN2 (Walden inversion); 2° halides can go either way depending on solvent/nucleophile strength.
  • Grignard reagent (RMgX) reacts by three broad pathways: (i) acid–base reaction with any active H (destroys the reagent), (ii) nucleophilic addition (NAR) to carbonyls, epoxides, CO₂, nitriles, (iii) nucleophilic substitution (NSR) with acid chlorides/esters.
  • RMgX + HCHO → 1° alcohol; + other aldehyde → 2° alcohol; + ketone → 3° alcohol; + CO₂ (then H₃O⁺) → carboxylic acid; + epoxide (then H⁺) → primary alcohol two carbons longer.
  • Wurtz reaction: 2 R–X + 2Na → R–R (best for making symmetrical alkanes; poor for unsymmetrical due to mixture of products).
  • Finkelstein reaction: R–Cl/Br + NaI (in dry acetone) → R–I (driven by NaCl/NaBr precipitating out of acetone).

6. Alcohol, Phenol & Ether

  • Acidity order: Carboxylic acid > Carbonic acid > Phenol > Water > Alcohol; phenol is acidic because the phenoxide ion is resonance-stabilised by the ring.
  • Dehydration (H₂SO₄/Δ) of alcohols follows the Saytzeff rule and proceeds through a carbocation (E1) — rearranged alkenes are possible.
  • Oxidation ladder: 1° alcohol → aldehyde → acid (with strong oxidants like K₂Cr₂O₇/H⁺); PCC or Collins reagent stops cleanly at the aldehyde. 2° alcohol → ketone (no further oxidation without C–C cleavage). 3° alcohol resists oxidation (no α-H on the carbinol carbon).
  • Williamson ether synthesis: sodium alkoxide/phenoxide + alkyl halide → ether (SN2 — the alkyl halide must be 1° for a good yield).
  • Reimer–Tiemann reaction: phenol + CHCl₃ + KOH (via dichlorocarbene) → ortho-hydroxybenzaldehyde (salicylaldehyde) as the major product.
  • Kolbe's reaction: sodium phenoxide + CO₂ (high pressure/temp) then H⁺ → salicylic acid (ortho product favoured, chelation-stabilised).

7. Carboxylic Acids

  • HVZ reaction (Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky): carboxylic acid with an α-H + Br₂/red P → α-bromo acid (proceeds via the acid's enol-like bromide intermediate).
  • Decarboxylation: sodium salt of a carboxylic acid + soda lime (NaOH + CaO), Δ → alkane one carbon shorter, losing CO₂.
  • Esterification (Fischer): RCOOH + R'OH ⇌ RCOOR' + H₂O, catalysed by conc. H₂SO₄; reversible, so excess alcohol or removal of water drives it forward.
  • Acidic strength order for substituted acids tracks the −I/−M effect of the substituent and its distance from −COOH: closer and stronger electron-withdrawing groups increase acidity.

8. Amines & Benzene Diazonium Chloride

  • Basicity in water: for methyl-substituted amines, 2° > 1° > 3° > NH₃ (steric hindrance + solvation effects on the 3° amine's lone pair); in the gas phase the order is purely +I driven: 3° > 2° > 1° > NH₃.
  • Aniline is far less basic than aliphatic amines — its nitrogen lone pair delocalises into the aromatic ring.
  • HNO₂ (NaNO₂ + HCl): 1° aliphatic amine → alcohol + N₂; aniline at 0–5 °C → benzene diazonium chloride (stable only in the cold); at higher temperature aniline gives phenol directly.
  • Diazonium salt reactions: Sandmeyer (CuCl/CuBr/CuCN, replaces N₂⁺ with Cl/Br/CN) and Gattermann (Cu/HX, same outcome, milder) both proceed via a radical pathway; with KI → iodobenzene needs no catalyst; with HBF₄ then Δ (Balz–Schiemann) → fluorobenzene; with H₃PO₂/H₂O → benzene (deamination); coupling with phenol/aniline (below 5 °C, mild base) → azo dye.
  • Hinsberg separation: benzenesulphonyl chloride distinguishes and can separate 1°, 2°, and 3° amines by their differing solubility behaviour with the sulphonamide product.

12. Carbohydrates

  • Carbohydrates are optically active polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones (or compounds that hydrolyse to give them).
  • Monosaccharide (CₙH₂ₙOₙ): a single unit that cannot be hydrolysed further into a simpler sugar.
  • Oligosaccharides: hydrolyse to give 2–10 monosaccharide units, linked by glycosidic bonds; the 2-unit case is a disaccharide.
  • Polysaccharide: hydrolyses to give more than ten monosaccharide units (e.g. starch, cellulose, glycogen).
  • Mutarotation: either pure anomeric form of D-glucose (α, [α]D = +111°, or β, [α]D = +19°), when dissolved in water, drifts to the same equilibrium specific rotation of about +52.5° as the ring opens and recloses.
  • Glucose gives a negative test with iodine solution (no blue colour) — unlike starch.
